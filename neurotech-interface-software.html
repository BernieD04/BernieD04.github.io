EEG and EMG Neural Interface Software

Aug 2024 - May 2025 Neurotech Club

A year on the Neurotech Club's neural-controlled assistive device, split between the EMG and EEG decoding and the actuator.

Someone with limited muscle strength often still produces the electrical command for a movement. The muscle fires and the signal reaches the skin; what is missing is the force at the end of it. A device that reads that signal and supplies the missing force is a different proposition from one driven by a button, because the intent is already in the body and only has to be recovered.

What I worked on

Two halves, at opposite ends of the same chain. On the software side I wrote the EEG and EMG preprocessing and decoding pipelines and trained the PyTorch models that classified the signals. On the hardware side I worked on the actuator, which is what turns a decoded intent into force on the limb. The decoding answers what the person is trying to do. The actuator is the part that does it.

The scope was in flux for most of the year, so what the device was meant to cover kept moving. The signal problem underneath it did not move: read intent off the body reliably enough that a motor can act on it, on a person who is not in a lab.

Why cross-session validation was most of the work

A substantial part of my time went to validating the pipelines across recording sessions rather than within one. That is a less interesting job than training the models and it matters more, because EEG and EMG recordings are not stable between sessions. Electrodes go back on in slightly different places, skin impedance changes between one day and the next, and the amplitude and spectrum of the signal move with them.

A model trained and tested inside one session learns those session-specific properties along with the thing it is supposed to classify, and scores well doing it. The same model can then fail on a recording taken from the same person a week later. For an assistive device that distinction is the whole question, since it has to work on the day it is worn and not only on the day it was fitted. Checking that results held across sessions is what separates a pipeline that works from one that looks like it works.

What it became

The project continued after I left and is now a company, Second Chance Innovations, building a powered upper-limb orthosis called the Rebo. It detects movement intent from EMG and supplies 10 to 15 pounds of lift assistance, aimed at people living with spinal cord injury and stroke. The narrowing to the upper limb, and the device settling into an orthosis that assists an arm rather than replacing one, happened after my time on it.

I was not part of that stage and the company's progress since is not mine to claim. What I can say is that the signal problem I spent the year on is the one the device still rests on.
